Video and Visual Representation

Unit 4 · topic 1 of 6

Video and Visual RepresentationWhat is video?

  • Video is a combination of image and audio. It consists of a set of still images called frames, displayed at a fixed speed called the frame rate (frames per second, fps).
  • Because of persistence of vision, the eye cannot separate the frames when they are shown fast enough and sees an illusion of motion.
  • A frame rate of 20 to 30 fps gives smooth, realistic motion.
  • Audio is added and synchronised with the apparent movement of the images.
  • In a conventional TV the signal is displayed on a cathode ray tube; an electron beam carries the pattern information (such as intensity) of the scene.
Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video and Visual RepresentationThree aspects of video signal representation

The goal of visual representation is to give the observer as realistic a picture of a scene as possible, so the picture must convey the spatial and temporal content of the scene accurately. DVI appears as the following measures.

Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video and Visual Representation1. Vertical detail and viewing distance (1/2)

  • Geometry of the picture follows the aspect ratio = picture width / height. For TV and old PC monitors it is 4/3 = 1.33; for widescreen it is 16/9.
  • Vertical detail is set by the number of scanning lines: more lines mean better vertical resolution (1080p has more vertical detail than 720p).
  • The full vertical resolution cannot be used because some details fall between scan lines. Experiments show the effective vertical resolution is about 70 % of the actual one. This factor is the Kell factor (0.7), independent of sequential or interlaced scanning.
\[\text{Effective vertical resolution} = \text{active scan lines} \times \text{Kell factor}\]
Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video and Visual Representation1. Vertical detail and viewing distance (2/2)

\[\text{Required horizontal resolution} = \text{aspect ratio} \times \text{effective vertical resolution}\]
Worked example (PAL)

Active lines = 576. Effective vertical = 576 × 0.7 = 403.2 lines. Required horizontal = 4/3 × 403.2 = 537.6.

2. Horizontal detail and picture width

  • Horizontal detail depends on the video bandwidth and the number of pixels per line; higher bandwidth gives better horizontal clarity.
  • The transmitted picture need not be the same size as the original, but the aspect ratio must be the same, otherwise the picture looks too wide or too narrow (stretched).
Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video and Visual Representation3. Total detail content

The total number of picture elements = vertical × horizontal elements. It measures the amount of information (texture, edges, fine detail). Complex images need higher resolution and higher bandwidth.

4. Perception of depth

  • In natural vision, depth comes mainly from the angular separation of the images seen by the two eyes.
  • On flat TV, depth is inferred from perspective, the focal length of the lens and the depth of focus. Shadows, motion and stereoscopic (3D) vision also help.
Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video and Visual Representation5. Luminance and chrominance

  • Colour is produced by three signals proportional to the red, green and blue intensities.
  • For transmission and storage a different split is used: one luminance (brightness) signal Y and two colour-difference (chrominance) signals U and V.
  • Y = 0.30R + 0.59G + 0.11B, U = c₁(B − Y), V = c₂(R − Y), where c₁ and c₂ are constants.
  • Luminance is measured in nits. Too high gives glare, too low a dull image.
  • Recall colour mixing: yellow + red = orange, yellow + blue = green.
Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video and Visual Representation6. Temporal aspects of illumination

  • The eye detects motion when slightly different still images follow each other rapidly.
  • This is the basis of cinema and TV.
  • Two conditions are needed: the rate of succession must be high, and vision must persist across the interval between flashes.
  • Sudden brightness changes cause discomfort, smooth transitions improve quality.
Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video and Visual Representation7. Continuity of motion

  • Frame rate = how often new display data is updated per second.
  • Motion is seen as continuous only above 15 fps; below that two frames can be told apart.
  • For smooth motion at least 30 fps are needed if the scene is filmed (not computer-generated). Cinema at 24 fps often looks strange when large objects move fast and close to the viewer.
  • NTSC: nominally 30, changed to 29.97 fps. PAL: 25 fps (25 Hz repetition).
Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video and Visual Representation8. Flicker (1/2)

  • Refresh rate = number of times the display redraws its scan lines per second. If it is too low, brightness fluctuates periodically: the flicker effect. A cinema film at 16 pictures per second flickers.
  • Minimum refresh rate to avoid flicker: 50 Hz.
  • Projectors show each frame 2 or 3 times, so 24 fps film runs at 48 or 72 Hz.

Two ways to cure flicker:

  1. Raise the refresh rate. Beyond a limit this may damage the hardware.
  2. Interlaced scanning: in the first pass the odd lines are drawn, in the second the even lines. Each frame has two fields and the refresh rate seen by the eye doubles.
Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video and Visual Representation8. Flicker (2/2)

625-line system

625 lines per frame → 312.5 lines per field, of which 292.5 are active (trace) lines and 20 are vertical retrace. The same holds for the second field.

Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video and Visual Representation9. Temporal aspect of video bandwidth (1/2)

  • The eye needs a frame scanned every 1/25 second; scan rate and resolution fix the bandwidth.
  • In one cycle of the video frequency two horizontally adjacent pixels can be scanned, hence the divide by 2.
\[\text{Horizontal scan frequency} = b \times c \qquad \text{Video bandwidth} = \frac{a \times b \times c}{2}\]

where a = pixels per line (horizontal), b = lines (vertical), c = frame rate.

Worked example

a = 1312, b = 800, c = 100 Hz: 1312 × 800 × 100 / 2 = 52,480,000 Hz = 52.48 MHz.

Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video and Visual Representation9. Temporal aspect of video bandwidth (2/2)

Extra

The list is not numbered consistently (a "4" may appear twice, and "8. Flicker" may follow "6."). The nine measures in the order above are the standard list. The flicker minimum is written as 50 cycles/s or 50 Hz. They are the same.

Exam tip

"Explain the visual representation of video" is usually answered with the headings above, one or two lines each. Always give the three numbers: 15 fps (continuity), 25/30 fps (smooth), 50 Hz (flicker).

Source note: Video and Visual Representation

Video Transmission and Digitization

Unit 4 · topic 2 of 6

Video Transmission and DigitizationTransmission standards and colour encoding

  • Transmission standards: NTSC (National Television System Committee) and PAL (Phase Alternating Line).
  • Colour encoding approaches: RGB, YUV, YIQ (together called component signals) and composite.
RGB YUV YIQ Composite
Signals Separate R, G, B Y + U + V Y + I + Q All in one signal
Used by Computer monitors PAL TV NTSC TV Broadcast, low-cost video
Strength Best colour reproduction Less bandwidth, backward compatible with black and white Uses eye's colour response One wire
Weakness Needs more bandwidth and synchronisation of 3 signals Needs conversion Needs conversion Interference between luminance and chrominance
Source note: Video Transmission and Digitization

Video Transmission and DigitizationRGB signal

  • Separate red, green and blue signals (three wires per pixel stream). Every colour is a combination of the three primaries by additive mixing.
  • Values are normalised so that R + G + B = 1 gives neutral white.
  • Used by computer monitors; best reproduction, but needs more bandwidth and tight synchronisation.
Source note: Video Transmission and Digitization

Video Transmission and DigitizationYUV signal (1/2)

  • The eye is more sensitive to brightness (luminance) than to colour (chrominance), so brightness is separated from colour.
\[Y = 0.30R + 0.59G + 0.11B$$ $$U = 0.493\,(B - Y) \qquad V = 0.877\,(R - Y)\]
  • Y is luminance, U is the blue chrominance, V is the red chrominance.
  • Because chrominance carries less information, U and V are scaled down (the 0.493 and 0.877 factors) to save bandwidth.
  • A black-and-white TV can use Y alone to show a colour broadcast.
  • YUV is sometimes described as using two wires (one for luminance, one for combined chrominance), gives less crosstalk and is also known as S-video.
Source note: Video Transmission and Digitization

Video Transmission and DigitizationYUV signal (2/2)

Watch out

The factors 0.493 and 0.877 are the usual scaling constants. The coefficients 0.30, 0.59, 0.11 sum to 1, as they must.

Source note: Video Transmission and Digitization

Video Transmission and DigitizationYIQ signal

  • Similar to YUV, used by the NTSC colour system. Y is luminance, I and Q are chrominance.
  • It takes advantage of human colour response: the eye is more sensitive to orange–blue (I) than to purple–green (Q), so Q needs less bandwidth.
Extra

The standard NTSC equations are Y = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B, I = 0.596R − 0.274G − 0.322B, Q = 0.211R − 0.523G + 0.312B. Slides often show them only as pictures, so learn the idea (Y plus two chrominance, Q needs least bandwidth) and quote the equations only if your teacher expects them.

Source note: Video Transmission and Digitization

Video Transmission and DigitizationComposite signal (1/2)

  • All information (luminance plus chrominance) is mixed into one signal on a carrier wave. The components RGB, YUV or YIQ must be combined.
  • Because they share one signal, interference may occur, so a suitable modulation is used. Example: in NTSC the chrominance signals I and Q are combined into a chroma signal kept at a high frequency within the channel shared with luminance Y.
  • Component (YUV) signals give better quality because colour and brightness stay separate.
Source note: Video Transmission and Digitization

Video Transmission and DigitizationComposite signal (2/2)

Source note: Video Transmission and Digitization

Video Transmission and DigitizationDigitization (1/2)

  • Digitization converts the analog video signal to digital form: better quality, easier storage and transmission.
  • Two steps: sampling digitizes the spatial position (divide the picture into an M × N array of points); quantizing digitizes the colour/brightness (divide the signal into a range of grey-level values).
  • Finally, the digital motion video is created by digitizing the pictures temporally (frame after frame).
Exam tip

A frequent 5-mark question: "Differentiate component and composite video." Component: separate R,G,B or Y,U,V or Y,I,Q signals, best quality, more bandwidth. Composite: one signal, cheap, interference between Y and chroma.

Source note: Video Transmission and Digitization

Video Transmission and DigitizationDigitization (2/2)

Extra

Do not confuse video digitization (sampling and quantizing) with image synthesis and analysis from Unit 3.

Source note: Video Transmission and Digitization

Computer Video Format

Unit 4 · topic 3 of 6

Computer Video FormatStandard for digitization: CCIR-601

The ITU-R (International Telecommunications Union – Radiocommunications Branch) defined Recommendation CCIR-601 to standardise digitization of video pictures. Variants exist for TV broadcasting, video telephony and videoconferencing.

Video digitizers differ in image resolution, quantization and frame rate. Example: SGI VINO digitizer for NTSC: 640 × 480, 8 bits/pixel, 4 frames/second.

Source note: Computer Video Format

Computer Video FormatRaster display architecture (1/2)

The output of digitized video depends on the input and output devices; the most common display is the raster display.

Source note: Computer Video Format

Computer Video FormatRaster display architecture (2/2)

  • The graphics program creates the high-level image interactively. The display controller interprets display commands and writes pixel values into the refresh buffer.
  • The video controller is hardware that reads the pixel values in time-synchronism with the scanning and converts them into red, green and blue analog signals for the display. It can also access memory.
Source note: Computer Video Format

Computer Video FormatColour look-up table (CLUT)

  • A range of colour values is stored in a table; each pixel value is an address into the table, which holds the corresponding three colour values.
  • This is the colour look-up table.
  • It saves frame-buffer memory and allows colour changes by editing the table (see Animation Basics).
Source note: Computer Video Format

Computer Video FormatVideo controller standards

CGA (Color Graphics Adapter) · EGA (Enhanced Graphics Adapter) · VGA (Video Graphics Array) · 8514/A display adapter mode · XGA (Extended Graphics Array) · Super VGA (SVGA).

Extra

The resolution table of these standards is often shown only as a picture. Commonly quoted: CGA 320 × 200 (4 colours), EGA 640 × 350 (16), VGA 640 × 480 (16) or 320 × 200 (256), SVGA 800 × 600 and above. Check against your textbook before quoting.

Computer Video FormatRaw video size (1/2)

\[\text{Frame size} = \text{width} \times \text{height} \times \text{bits per pixel}$$ $$\text{Data rate} = \text{frame size} \times \text{frame rate}\]
Worked example: PAL, 352 × 288, 16 bits/pixel, 25 fps
  • Frame size = 352 × 288 × 16 = 1,622,016 bits (= 202,752 bytes, about 202.5 KB with decimal kilobytes).
  • Data rate = 1,622,016 × 25 = 40,550,400 bits/s.
  • In MB/s: 40,550,400 ÷ (8 × 1024 × 1024) = 4.83 MB/s.
Units

Be consistent: state whether you use 1000 or 1024 for KB and MB. For example 202,752 bytes ÷ 1024 = 198 KB.

Source note: Computer Video Format

Computer Video FormatRaw video size (2/2)

Quick practice

640 × 480, 24-bit, 30 fps: frame = 921,600 bytes; rate = 27,648,000 bytes/s ≈ 26.4 MB/s; one minute ≈ 1.55 GB.

Animation Basics

Unit 4 · topic 4 of 6

Animation BasicsWhat is animation?

  • Animation is making something look and feel alive: it covers all changes that have a visual effect (time-varying position, shape, colour, transparency, structure, texture).
  • Computer-based animation is done with a computer having graphical tools; similar images are displayed quickly. It is based on the keyframe concept: a keyframe holds the positions (begin and end of a transition) of the objects.

Two categories:

Motion dynamics Update dynamics
Induces life by Changing the position of an object Changing the shape, size or structure
Example Bouncing ball Flower blooming from a bud
Source note: Animation Basics

Animation BasicsStages of the process (1/2)

Source note: Animation Basics

Animation BasicsStages of the process (2/2)

  1. Input process: drawings must be digitized (optical scanning, data tablet or drawing program) and filtered to clean up glitches.
  2. Composition stage: separate elements (foreground characters, background scenery, special effects) are combined into the final frames.
  3. Pencil test: checks the animation is correct, using an animation camera, before cleaning and painting the drawings. Pan and zoom effects slowly zoom in on a subject and pan between subjects.
  4. In-between process: frames with intermediate positions between key frames are composed.
  5. Changing colours: using the CLUT.
Source note: Animation Basics

Animation BasicsIn-betweening and interpolation

  • Computer in-betweening uses interpolation (lerping, linear interpolation): given start and end positions, intermediate positions are computed on a straight line.
  • Lerp is the easiest method but gives unrealistic motion. Example: a ball thrown in the air with only three key frames would move in straight segments instead of an arc.
  • Splines are used to smooth the interpolation between key frames.
\[P(t) = (1-t)\,P_{start} + t\,P_{end}, \quad 0 \le t \le 1\]
Extra

The lerp formula is standard. Example: start x = 0, end x = 100, 5 in-betweens means t = 1/6, 2/6, …: x ≈ 16.7, 33.3, 50, 66.7, 83.3.

Source note: Animation Basics

Animation BasicsChanging colours with a CLUT

  • Colours are changed by manipulating the colour look-up table in the frame buffer. The simplest method is to cycle the colours in the table, changing the colour of various parts of the image.
  • CLUT animation is faster than sending a whole new pixmap to the frame buffer for every frame.
Watch out

Do not mix up the input process (digitizing drawings) with in-betweening (computing intermediate frames).

Exam tip

Typical questions: "What is in-betweening? Why are splines used?" and "Explain CLUT animation." Give the lerp weakness (ball path) explicitly.

Source note: Animation Basics

Animation BasicsCheckpoint (1/2)

Check yourself

Motion dynamics changes position (bouncing ball); update dynamics changes shape, size or structure (flower blooming).

Check yourself

Source note: Animation Basics

Animation Languages and Control

Unit 4 · topic 5 of 6

Animation languages (three categories)1. Linear-list notations

Text-based scripts where each event gives start frame, end frame and action, written sequentially like a list. Easy to follow; used in early multimedia and timeline-based engines.

Example

42, 53, B, ROTATE "PALM", 1, 30 means: between frames 42 and 53 rotate the object PALM about axis 1 by 30 degrees, the amount per frame being taken from table B.

Examples: Scefo (SCEne FOrmat); SMIL (Synchronized Multimedia Integration Language) with <seq> and <par>; a traffic light script: 00s setColor light RED, 05s … YELLOW, 07s … GREEN, 12s … RED.

Source note: Animation Languages and Control

Animation languages (three categories)2. General-purpose languages

Animation capability embedded in a general programming language; variables of the language serve as parameters of animation routines. Example: ASAS, built on LISP, with many geometric transformations.

3. Graphical languages

Textual languages cannot visualise action. Graphical languages let the animator provide a picture of the action, to express, edit and understand simultaneous changes. Examples: GENESYS, DIAL, S-Dynamics System.

Category Describes by Example
Linear list Event list with frame numbers Scefo, SMIL
General purpose Program code ASAS
Graphical Pictures of actions GENESYS, DIAL, S-Dynamics
Source note: Animation Languages and Control

Source note: Animation Languages and Control

Methods of controlling animationTracking live action

  • Rotoscoping: film of people or animals acting the parts; animators draw over the film, replacing the actors with animated equivalents.
  • Indicators on key body points: tracked positions give locations for the matching points of an animated model. A data glove measures hand position and orientation and the flexion and hyperextension of each finger.
Source note: Animation Languages and Control

Methods of controlling animationKinematics vs dynamics

  • Kinematics: position and velocity of points. "The cube is at the origin at t = 0 and moves with constant acceleration in direction (1, 1, 5)."
  • Dynamics: includes the physical laws (Newton): acceleration is proportional to force, the constant being the mass. "At t = 0 the cube is at (0, 100, 0) m, has mass 100 g, and gravity acts on it."
  • Knowing kinematics and dynamics at a point lets us determine the next point of the motion.
Exam tip

"Explain any three methods of controlling animation" is common: pick explicit, procedural and constraint-based, with one example each.

Animation Display and Transmission

Unit 4 · topic 6 of 6

Animation Display and TransmissionDisplay of animation

  • Animated objects (lines, polygons, …) must be scan-converted into a pixmap in the frame buffer. To show a rotating object, successive views from slightly different positions are scan-converted one after another.
  • A raster screen sweeps one row at a time, top to bottom; the refresh buffer holds the intensity values for the next picture.
  • Scan conversion must be done at least 10 (preferably 15 to 20) times per second, so a new image must be created in no more than 100 ms.
  • Erasing and redrawing within that time is impossible with a single buffer; it gives a distracting effect.
Source note: Animation Display and Transmission

Display of animationDouble buffering

  • Solution: two buffers (the two halves of the pixmap: image0 and image1). One is displayed while the next frame is built in the other; then they swap.
  • Increases throughput and gives more realistic animation.
Source note: Animation Display and Transmission

Animation Display and TransmissionTransmission of animation (1/2)

Two approaches:

  • A pixmap is a rectangular array of pixel colour values; with 1 bit per pixel it is a bitmap.
Pixmap rate

320 × 240, 8 bits, 15 images/s: 76,800 bytes × 15 = 1,152,000 bytes/s ≈ 1.1 MB/s.

Why is double buffering needed?

A single buffer cannot erase, redraw and show a frame within 100 ms (or 50 ms), causing distracting effects; two buffers allow one to display while the other is filled.
