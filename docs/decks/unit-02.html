Unit 2Overview

Unit 1 told you what multimedia is. This unit starts the tour of the individual media with the one you can hear: sound. You will learn what sound physically is, how a computer turns it into numbers, how music is described as instructions (MIDI) instead of samples, and how computers generate, analyse and transmit human speech.

Unit 2 objectivesWhat you must master

  1. Define sound, waveform, period, frequency and amplitude, and give the frequency ranges.
  2. Distinguish sound from audio.
  3. Explain how an analog sound wave becomes digital: sampling, the Nyquist theorem and quantization, and calculate file size with the formula.
  4. Describe MIDI: the two parts of the interface, messages, devices, channels, and the MIDI versus SMPTE timing difference.
  5. Explain speech generation (time-dependent and frequency-dependent concatenation, formants) and the two steps of speech synthesis.
  6. Explain speech analysis (who, what, how) and speech recognition.
  7. Explain speech transmission (PCM, source encoding, recognition/synthesis) and compare the common audio file formats.

Basic Sound Concepts

Unit 2 · topic 1 of 6

Basic Sound ConceptsIn plain words

The idea

Sound is vibration of the air. A microphone turns it into an electrical signal. A bigger wave is louder; a faster wave is higher in pitch.

Real-life picture

Pluck a guitar string harder and it gets louder (amplitude); press it on a fret to shorten it and it sounds higher (frequency).

Remember it

Amplitude decides loudness, frequency decides pitch. Humans hear about 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Frequency f = 1 / T.

Basic Sound ConceptsWhat is sound?

Sound is the physical phenomenon produced by the vibration of matter.

When matter vibrates, pressure variations are created in the surrounding air. This alternation of high and low pressure travels through the air in a wave-like motion. When the wave reaches the human ear, a sound is heard.

Source note: Basic Sound Concepts

Basic Sound ConceptsWaveform and period

  • The pattern of the oscillation is called a waveform.
  • The waveform repeats the same shape at regular intervals. One such interval is called a period.
  • Natural sound waves are never perfectly smooth or uniformly periodic.

A typical diagram shows air pressure plotted against time as a smooth wave: the vertical distance from the middle line to a peak is the amplitude, and the horizontal distance of one full repeat is one period.

Source note: Basic Sound Concepts

Frequency

The frequency of a sound is the reciprocal of the period. It is the number of periods in one second, measured in hertz (Hz) or cycles per second (cps).

Basic Sound ConceptsAmplitude (1/2)

  • The amplitude is the measure of the displacement of the air pressure wave from its mean (quiescent or idle) value.
  • The greater the amplitude, the louder the sound.

Digital Audio and File Formats

Unit 2 · topic 2 of 6

Digital Audio and File FormatsIn plain words

The idea

To digitise sound the computer measures the wave many times per second (sampling) and rounds each measurement to one of a fixed number of levels (quantization).

Real-life picture

An audio CD takes 44,100 measurements every second and stores each one in 16 bits for each of two channels.

Remember it

Sample at least twice the highest frequency (Nyquist). Size = rate × (bits/8) × channels × seconds.

Digital Audio and File FormatsAnalog to digital: sample, quantize, encode

The first 8 samples are encoded as 3-bit PCM words on the right.

Digital Audio and File FormatsComputer representation of sound (1/2)

The smooth, continuous curve of a sound wave cannot be stored directly in a computer. Instead:

  1. The computer measures the amplitude of the waveform at regular time intervals.
  2. This produces a series of numbers called samples.
  3. The conversion is done by an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC).
  4. The reverse is done by a Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC).

An example of an ADC is the AM79C30A digital subscriber controller chip. A typical diagram shows the smooth pressure wave on the left and, on the right, the same wave as a row of vertical bars (the samples) whose heights follow the wave.

Source note: Digital Audio and File Formats

Digital Audio and File FormatsComputer representation of sound (2/2)

Source note: Digital Audio and File Formats

Digital Audio and File FormatsSampling rate

The rate at which a continuous waveform is sampled is the sampling rate, measured in hertz.

Nyquist sampling theorem

For lossless digitization, the sampling rate should be at least twice the maximum frequency response.

Example. The CD standard sampling rate is 44,100 Hz: the waveform is sampled 44,100 times per second. A rate of 44,100 Hz can only represent frequencies up to 22,050 Hz, which is why it covers the audible range up to 20 kHz.

Digital Audio and File FormatsQuantization

The value of each sample must be stored as a discrete number. The resolution (quantization) of a sample depends on the number of bits used to measure the height of the waveform.

Bits per sample Number of levels Example
3-bit 2³ = 8 An illustration of coarse quantization
8-bit 2⁸ = 256 Lower quality
16-bit 2¹⁶ = 65,536 CD quality

The lower the quantization, the lower the quality of the sound.

MIDI

Unit 2 · topic 3 of 6

MIDIIn plain words

The idea

MIDI is sheet music for machines: it sends which note to play, how hard and on which instrument, not the sound itself.

Real-life picture

A MIDI keyboard connected to a laptop: the keys send small messages and the laptop's software instruments make the sound.

Remember it

MIDI files are tiny and easy to edit, but they need a synthesizer to be heard. Note On = status byte + key number + velocity.

MIDIWhy MIDI exists (1/2)

  • A sound signal can be stored as a digitised sequence of samples.
  • But music can also be described in a symbolic way, the way a printed score lists notes.
  • Computers and electronic musical instruments use a similar technique, and most of them use MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface), a standard developed in the early 1980s.
Source note: MIDI

MIDIWhy MIDI exists (2/2)

  • The MIDI standard defines how to code all the elements of a musical score: sequences of notes, timing conditions, and the instrument that plays each note.
  • It is a set of specifications for instrument development, so instruments from different manufacturers can easily exchange musical information.
  • Each word describing an action in a musical performance is assigned a specific binary code.
Samples versus instructions

Digitised audio stores what the sound looked like (thousands of sample values per second). MIDI stores what to play (which note, how loud, on which instrument). That is why a MIDI file is tiny and why it contains no individual sample values.

Source note: MIDI

MIDIThe two components of a MIDI interface

Component What it does
Hardware Connects the equipment. It adds a MIDI port to an instrument, specifies a MIDI cable, and processes the electrical signals over the cable.
Data format Encodes the information the hardware processes. It does not encode individual sampling values; instead it uses a specific data format for each instrument, covering the start and end of a score, the basic frequency, the loudness and the instrument itself.

A typical diagram shows a laptop (the digital audio workstation) connected to a synthesizer, a MIDI keyboard and an audio interface with USB, audio and MIDI cables.

Source note: MIDI

MIDIMIDI messages

  • The MIDI data format is digital, and data are grouped into MIDI messages.
  • When a musician plays a key, the MIDI interface generates a message that defines the start of each note and its intensity.
  • The message is transmitted to the machines connected to the system.
Source note: MIDI

MIDIMIDI devices, channels and instruments

  • An instrument that complies with both components of the MIDI interface (hardware and data format) is a MIDI device. Example: a synthesizer.
  • The MIDI standard specifies 16 channels. A MIDI device is mapped onto a channel.
  • The standard identifies 128 instruments by number, including noise effects such as a phone ringing or an airplane taking off.

MIDI and SMPTE timingMIDI clock

  • Used to synchronise timing between MIDI devices.
  • It keeps all connected devices playing in time with each other, which is crucial for rhythm and tempo in live performances and recordings.
  • As an alternative, the SMPTE clock allows receiver-sender synchronisation.
A number that does not match the MIDI standard

Some sources say the MIDI clock sends 2 timing ticks per quarter note. The MIDI specification itself defines 24 timing clocks per quarter note. Always quote the standard figure of 24 in exams.

Source note: MIDI

MIDI and SMPTE timingSMPTE timing code (1/2)

  • Developed by the Society of Motion Picture and Television Engineers.
  • Uses the format hours:minutes:seconds:frames.
  • Typically used in film and television to precisely synchronise audio and video.
  • Requires more bandwidth than a MIDI connection can support, because it carries detailed timing information for each frame.

Format: HH:MM:SS:FF, where HH = hours, MM = minutes, SS = seconds, FF = frames.

Speech Generation

Unit 2 · topic 4 of 6

Speech GenerationIn plain words

The idea

Computers create speech by joining small recorded pieces (phones, diphones, syllables) or by computing the sounds with a model of the vocal tract.

Real-life picture

A navigation app speaking street names, or a screen reader reading a web page aloud.

Remember it

Two steps of synthesis: text is transcribed into a sound script, then the sounds are synthesised into speech.

Speech GenerationAbout speech

Speech is the vocalised form of human communication. It can be perceived, understood and generated by humans and also by machines.

  • A human adjusts very efficiently to different speakers and their speech habits.
  • The brain can recognise the very fine line between speech and noise.
  • The human speech signal contains a subjective lowest spectral component called the pitch, which is not proportional to frequency.

Speech generation is the computer-generated simulation of human speech. Generated speech must be understandable and must sound natural.

Source note: Speech Generation

Reproduced speech output: two methodsTime-dependent sound concatenation (1/2)

Pre-recorded or pre-synthesised sound segments are pieced together based on their timing. Individual speech units are composed like building blocks, and the composition can happen at different levels.

  1. Phones. With just a few phones it is possible to create an unlimited vocabulary.
  2. Diphones. Two phones form a diphone. For example, the word crumb is built from an ordered sequence of diphones.
  3. Syllables. To make the transition problem easier, syllables can be created, and speech is generated from a set of syllables (for example crumb as one syllable sound).
  4. Whole words. The best pronunciation of a word comes from storing the whole word. This leads to synthesis of the whole speech sequence.
Source note: Speech Generation

Reproduced speech output: two methodsTime-dependent sound concatenation (2/2)

Assessment: often used in high-quality text-to-speech (TTS) systems for its naturalness, but it needs extensive databases and careful management of transitions.

Frequency-dependent sound concatenation

Speech is generated by manipulating the frequency components of sound, for example through formant synthesis.

  • Formants are frequency maxima in the spectrum of the speech signal.
  • Formant synthesis uses mathematical models of the vocal tract to generate speech sounds by controlling the frequency and amplitude of the formants (resonant frequencies).

Assessment: offers more flexibility and needs less storage, but can struggle to sound natural.

Speech Analysis

Speech AnalysisIn plain words

The idea

Speech analysis asks three questions about a voice: who is speaking, what was said and how was it said?

Real-life picture

Voice unlock (who), voice typing on your phone (what), a call-centre tool that senses an angry caller (how).

Remember it

Who = verification and identification. What = recognition and understanding. How = emotion and state of the speaker.

Speech AnalysisSpeech Analysis

Speech analysis (speech input) asks three questions about a spoken signal. It is often drawn as a tree: Speech analysis splits into Who? (verification and identification), What? (recognition and understanding) and How?.

Source note: Speech Analysis

Speech AnalysisWho is speaking?

Human speech has characteristics that are determined by the speaker. Speech analysis can therefore recognise a speaker for identification and verification.

  • The computer identifies and verifies the speaker using an acoustic fingerprint.
  • An acoustic fingerprint is a digitally stored speech probe of a person (for example, a certain statement).
Source note: Speech Analysis

Speech AnalysisWhat has been said?

This task analyses what was said: to recognise and understand the speech signal itself. A corresponding text is generated from the speech sequence. Applications:

  • A speech-controlled typewriter
  • A translation system
  • Part of a workplace for the handicapped
Source note: Speech Analysis

Speech AnalysisHow was it said?

This area researches the speech pattern of a statement: how a certain sentence was said. A spoken sentence sounds different if a person is angry or calm.

Question Task Example

What? Recognition and understanding of the content Speech-controlled typewriter, translation

Speech AnalysisSpeech recognition

Speech recognition is the process of converting an acoustic signal, captured by a microphone or a telephone, to a set of words (that is, spoken language to written text or a similar form). It is the foundation of human-computer interaction using speech.

Recognition systems differ along four contexts:

Context Options
The speaker Speaker-dependent or speaker-independent
The speech Discrete words or continuous speech
The vocabulary Small or large
The environment Quiet or noisy

Speech Transmission

Unit 2 · topic 6 of 6

Speech TransmissionIn plain words

The idea

Sending speech costs bits. PCM keeps everything, source encoding uses properties of speech to save bits, and recognition plus synthesis sends only text.

Real-life picture

A normal phone call uses 64 kbit/s (8,000 samples × 8 bits); a good voice codec can use much less.

Remember it

PCM = accurate but big. Source encoding = smaller. Recognise-then-synthesise = smallest, but the original voice is lost.

Speech TransmissionSpeech Transmission

Speech transmission is the field of highly efficient encoding of speech signals so they can be sent at a low data rate while keeping noticeable quality loss to a minimum.

There are three principles:

  1. Pulse Code Modulation (PCM)
  2. Source coding
  3. Recognition and synthesis methods
Source note: Speech Transmission

Speech Transmission1. Pulse Code Modulation (PCM)

  • Signal-form encoding does not consider speech-specific properties or parameters. The techniques are simply expected to encode audio signals efficiently.
  • A straightforward technique for digitising an analog signal (waveform) is PCM.
  • PCM prioritises the accurate representation of the audio signal over data reduction.
Source note: Speech Transmission

Speech Transmission2. Source encoding (1/2)

An alternative method: some transformations depend on the original signal type. An audio signal has certain characteristics that can be exploited in compression.

  • Suppressing silence in speech sequences is a typical example. It depends entirely on the meaning (semantics) of the signal.
  • Parametric systems use source encoding. They use speech-specific characteristics to reduce data, for example the channel vocoder.

Speech Transmission3. Recognition / synthesis method

  • Speech analysis (recognition) takes place on the sender side of the transmission system.
  • Speech synthesis (generation) takes place on the receiver side.
  • Only the characteristics of the speech elements are transmitted, not the waveform.
