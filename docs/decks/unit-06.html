Basic Technology

Unit 6 · topic 1 of 6

Basic TechnologyIn plain words

The idea

A compact disc stores bits as microscopic pits along one long spiral track. A laser shines on the track and a sensor reads changes in the reflected light.

Real-life picture

A tiny, very precise version of a vinyl record read with light instead of a needle.

Remember it

Three layers: polycarbonate substrate, reflective layer, protective layer. A change between pit and land represents a 1.

Basic TechnologyPits and lands

  • Information is stored in a spiral-shaped track as a series of pits and lands in the substrate layer.
  • A transition from pit to land or land to pit = '1'; between transitions a run of '0's. (This is differential NRZ coding.)
  • Track density about 16,000 turns per inch (tpi): track pitch 1.6 µm (a floppy disk has 96 tpi).
  • The laser is focused on the reflective layer: lands reflect almost totally, pits scatter the light, so the reflected light level changes.
Check

25.4 mm ÷ 1.6 µm = 15,875 ≈ 16,000 tpi.

Source note: Basic Technology

Basic TechnologyPhysical structure (three layers) (1/2)

  1. Transparent substrate (polycarbonate) with pits and lands, transparent for the laser beam.
  2. Reflective layer (aluminium, very thin).
  3. Protective layer (polycarbonate, used as carrier) with the label.
Source note: Basic Technology

Basic TechnologyPhysical structure (three layers) (2/2)

Disc thickness about 1.2 mm.

Player mechanism

A laser beam is reflected from below the disc; a prism diverts the reflected beam to a photo cell; a lens focuses the beam. The wavelength is about 780 nm and it can be focused on a spot of about 1 µm.

Video Disks: Laser Vision, DVD and Blu-rayIn plain words

The idea

Video discs went from analog LaserDisc to digital DVD and then Blu-ray, using shorter laser wavelengths and tighter tracks to store more.

Real-life picture

A DVD movie holds about 4.7 GB; a Blu-ray movie disc holds 25 GB per layer.

Remember it

Shorter wavelength + smaller pits + tighter tracks = more capacity: CD 780 nm, DVD 650/635 nm, Blu-ray 405 nm.

CD Audio (CD-DA)

Unit 6 · topic 3 of 6

CD Audio (CD-DA)In plain words

The idea

CD-DA stores stereo sound as 44.1 kHz, 16-bit PCM. Heavy error correction (CIRC) and EFM coding make it robust against scratches.

Real-life picture

A scratched CD can still play because the player rebuilds lost data from redundant bits.

Remember it

Red Book. 44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bit/s. 74 min = 333,000 blocks of 2,352 bytes.

CD Audio (CD-DA)Characteristics (1/2)

Capacity (no error correction) 74 × 60 × 1,411,200 = 6,265,728,000 bit ≈ 747 MB

Pit and land lengths are multiples of 0.3 µm.

Source note: CD Audio (CD-DA)

CD Audio (CD-DA)Characteristics (2/2)

Check

6,265,728,000 ÷ 8 = 783,216,000 bytes; ÷ 1,048,576 = 746.9 MB.

Source note: CD Audio (CD-DA)

CD Audio (CD-DA)Eight-to-Fourteen Modulation (EFM)

Why a special code?

  • The laser has limited resolution: transitions cannot be too close, so at least two 0s between two 1s.
  • The clock is recovered from the transitions: not more than 10 consecutive 0s.

EFM: each 8-bit value is coded with 14 bits. 267 fourteen-bit patterns satisfy the rules and 256 are used (those cheapest to implement in gates).

When 14-bit values are concatenated the rules could be broken, so three merging bits are added between them. Each byte therefore uses 17 channel bits (14 + 3).

Exam tip

Study the rule (min 2 zeros, max 10 zeros, 3 merging bits) rather than memorising one example.

Source note: CD Audio (CD-DA)

CD Audio (CD-DA)Error handling

Scratches, dust and fingerprints cause burst errors, which must be detected and corrected. Re-sending (ARQ) is impossible on a disc, so forward error correction (FEC) is used.

Source note: CD Audio (CD-DA)

Error handlingCIRC: Cross Interleaved Reed-Solomon Code (1/3)

Two levels:

  1. Reed-Solomon at byte level (EDC and ECC): two groups, each with 4 correction bytes for 24 data bytes. The first group corrects single-byte errors; the second corrects double-byte errors and detects further errors.
  2. Frame interleaving: consecutive data bytes and their ECC bytes are spread over adjacent frames, turning a burst into scattered small errors.

Performance: error rate about 10⁻⁸ (about 1 bit in 100 million); bursts of 4,000 data bits (about 2.5 mm) are corrected exactly; errors up to 12,300 data bits (about 7 mm) are repaired by interpolation.

Source note: CD Audio (CD-DA)

Error handlingCIRC: Cross Interleaved Reed-Solomon Code (2/3)

Watch out

A common calculation multiplies 4,000 × 0.3 µm and writes "about 2.5 mm". The product is 1.2 mm; the 2.5 mm and 7 mm figures are the usual quoted values. Learn the figures 4,000 bits/2.5 mm and 12,300 bits/7 mm.

Source note: CD Audio (CD-DA)

Error handlingCIRC: Cross Interleaved Reed-Solomon Code (3/3)

Extra: error-control theory
  • Error detection with a polynomial: treat data as A(x), divide by generator G(x), send the remainder; the receiver divides again. A parity bit (G(x) = x + 1) detects an odd number of errors but misses an even number.
  • FEC works by choosing codewords with large Hamming distance: if the minimum distance is 2t + 1, any error of t bits or fewer can be corrected.
  • 2-D parity (row and column) can locate and correct a single error, but several errors can produce a wrong "correction".
  • For many receivers an XOR repair packet (c = a₁ ⊕ a₂ ⊕ … ⊕ aₙ) lets any receiver that lost one packet rebuild it.
Source note: CD Audio (CD-DA)

CD Audio (CD-DA)Frames

One frame:

Part Content
Audio data 2 groups of 12 bytes (24 bytes)
Error detection/correction 2 groups of 4 parity bytes (8 bytes, Reed-Solomon)
Control and display 1 byte; its bits are called P, Q, R, S, T, U, V, W; the same bit of 98 frames forms a subchannel (e.g. subchannel P marks audio versus data, Q holds the table of contents and time)
Synchronization pattern 12 ones + 12 zeros + 3 merging bits = 27 bits, a unique pattern marking the frame start

Each frame has 24 + 8 + 1 = 33 bytes, each coded in 17 bits = 561 bits, plus the 27-bit sync = 588 channel bits.

Source note: CD Audio (CD-DA)

CD Audio (CD-DA)Three data streams

Stream Content Rate
Audio bit stream 16-bit stereo PCM, 44.1 kHz 1.41 × 10⁶ bit/s (1,411,200)

Check

Frames per second = 176,400 ÷ 24 = 7,350. Data: 7,350 × 33 × 8 = 1,940,400 bit/s. Channel: 7,350 × 588 = 4,321,800 bit/s.

Source note: CD Audio (CD-DA)

CD Audio (CD-DA)Areas and random access

  • Lead-in area: table of contents, start of each track.
  • Program area: up to 99 tracks, typically one song per track.
  • Lead-out area: lets the player leave the program area in a controlled way.
  • Index points: IP0 = start of track, IP1 = start of audio data; the part between them is the track pregap.
Source note: CD Audio (CD-DA)

CD Audio (CD-DA)Summary and limits

CD-DA suits typical damage in audio and is the base of the whole CD family. But it was not conceived for video (different ECC/EDC needed) or discrete data (error rate too high), and does not allow simultaneous playback of several media.

Exam tip

Learn the three numbers 1,411,200 bit/s, 74 min ≈ 747 MB and 588 bits per frame, and the two EFM rules. CIRC is always "Reed-Solomon in two levels plus interleaving".

Source note: CD Audio (CD-DA)

CD Audio (CD-DA)Checkpoint

Check yourself

Why is EFM used?

To keep at least two and at most ten zeros between ones so the laser can resolve transitions and the clock can be recovered.

Check yourself

Why interleaving?

Burst errors hit consecutive bytes; spreading bytes over adjacent frames leaves only a few bytes in error per code word, which Reed-Solomon can correct.

CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XA

Unit 6 · topic 4 of 6

CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XAIn plain words

The idea

CD-ROM adds an extra layer of error correction so computers can read data without a single wrong bit. Mode 1 is for data, Mode 2 for audio and video.

Real-life picture

Installing software from a disc needs perfect data; one wrong bit could break a program.

Remember it

Yellow Book. Mode 1 = 2,048 user bytes + EDC/ECC. Mode 2 = 2,336 user bytes.

CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XAWhy CD-ROM differs from CD-DA

Computer data is much more sensitive to errors than audio (no repetition, interpolation or dropping possible) and needs smaller units for random access. So CD-ROM adds extra error correction and uses blocks (sectors) instead of whole tracks.

Standard: Yellow Book

  • Mode 1: for any data (computer data).
  • Mode 2: for compressed audio and video data.
  • They cannot be combined within a single track; a track holds only CD-DA audio or only CD-ROM data.
  • Mixed-mode disc: data tracks first, then audio tracks.
Source note: CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XA

CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XAStructure: from audio bits to blocks (1/2)

  • 75 blocks per second (single speed). 1,411,200 ÷ 75 ÷ 8 = 2,352 bytes per block.
  • 74 min × 60 × 75 = 333,000 blocks.
Source note: CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XA

CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XAStructure: from audio bits to blocks (2/2)

Watch out
  • A block holds 98 frames (2,352 ÷ 24 audio bytes per frame; 98 × 588 channel bits = 57,624 bits = 7,203 bytes).
  • Do not round the block count to 330,000: it is exactly 333,000.
Source note: CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XA

CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XAAverage access time

  • Synchronization time: adapt the internal clock (milliseconds).
  • Seek time: move the laser to the radius, at most 1 s.
  • Rotation delay (CLV): find the sector and adapt disc speed, about 300 ms.
  • Maximum access time > 1 s; average > 300 ms (with caching). Reading audio and other data simultaneously in mode 1 is not possible.
Source note: CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XA

CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XAFile system

  • CD-ROM itself has no logical file format and no directory specification.
  • High Sierra proposal: industry group, the first file system, leading to ISO 9660.
  • ISO 9660: directory tree with file information; path table (list of all directories, direct access to files at any level); file interleaving.
  • First track: 16 blocks (sectors 0–15) system area, then volume descriptors: primary descriptor (length of file system, length and address of path table), supplementary descriptors, and a volume descriptor terminator.
  • Logical block size 512, 1,024 or 2,048 bytes (powers of 2); files begin at the start of a logical block.
Source note: CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XA

CD-ROM and CD-ROM/XACD-ROM Extended Architecture (CD-ROM/XA)

  • Announced 1988 by Philips, Sony and Microsoft. Goal: simultaneous transfer of various media.
  • Based on CD-ROM mode 2, ISO 9660 and CD-I; an extension of the Yellow Book.
  • Blocks of different media are interleaved in the same track; a new track type carries compressed audio (ADPCM), video, images, text and programs.

The idea

CD-I is a disc and player system for interactive multimedia. Photo CD stores each photo in several resolutions.

Real-life picture

Photo CD lets you view a quick small preview or load the full high-resolution scan.

CD-I, CD-I Ready, Bridge and Photo CDCD-Interactive (CD-I)

CD-WO and CD-MO

Unit 6 · topic 6 of 6

CD-WO and CD-MOIn plain words

The idea

CD-WO lets you record once; CD-MO can be erased and written again using heat and a magnetic field.

Real-life picture

Burning a backup disc once (WO) versus a reusable magneto-optical cartridge (MO).

Remember it

Orange Book covers both: WO = write once, MO = magneto-optical rewritable.

CD-WO and CD-MOCD-WO and CD-MO

Both are defined in the Orange Book: Part II = CD-WO, Part I = CD-MO. They extend the CD family from read-only (RO) to write-once (WO) and read/write (R/W).

Source note: CD-WO and CD-MO

CD-WO and CD-MOCD Write-Once (CD-WO) (1/2)

Layers (from the top): protective layer, reflective layer, absorption layer, substrate (with a pre-grooved track).

Principle

  • A pre-grooved track guides the writing laser.
  • An absorption layer sits between the substrate and the reflective layer.
  • A stronger write laser heats the absorption layer, causing an irreversible change of its reflection characteristics, which acts as the pits. The data cannot be erased.
  • A CD-WO can be played in CD-DA players.

Sessions

A CD player needs a table of contents. A CD-WO is written in sessions, each with a lead-in, data part and lead-out; new sessions can be added, up to 99 sessions.

Principle of the magneto-optical technique

Operation How
Write Heat a block and apply a magnetic field (about 10 × the earth's field); the single elements are polarized
Erase Apply a constant magnetic field while heating the block
Read The polarization of the light is influenced by the magnetic properties of the surface

  • Optional premastered area: read-only, readable on any CD player, uses Red, Yellow and Green Book specifications.
  • Rewritable area: uses different magneto-optical encoding, incompatible with other CDs and cannot be read by standard CD players.

CD-WO and CD-MOOverall summary of the CD family (1/2)

  • Features: usable for all kinds of storage, wide application area, large capacity, based on CD-DA (except CD-MO), specifications built sequentially.
  • Disadvantages: long average access time; incompatibility of CD-MO.
  • Future (the common view): CDs with more storage and faster retrieval, and smaller discs with similar capacity.

Why can a CD-WO not be rewritten?

Why can't standard CD players read the rewritable area of a CD-MO?

It uses magneto-optical encoding (polarization of light) unlike pits and lands.
